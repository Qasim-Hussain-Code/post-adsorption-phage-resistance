  This script was written to test the idea that part of the unexplained failure is lysogeny rather than host resistance. The answer separates cleanly into a direction and a magnitude.
  The direction supports it. Phages carrying a tyrosine integrase form plaques on 0.9 percent of the strains they met, against 21.2 percent for the rest. A phage that can integrate is far less likely to leave a plaque, which is what lysogeny predicts, and the genera involved are Lambdavirus, Wanchaivirus.
  The magnitude does not. Only 2 of 96 phages carry the marker, and they account for 793 of 30459 failures (2.6 percent). Even counting every one of those as a silent lysogenisation rather than a resistance event moves the overall failure rate only from 79.25 to 78.83 percent.
  Lysogeny is therefore a real but marginal explanation in this collection. The Guelin phages were selected for lytic activity, so temperate ones are rare by construction, and the unexplained remainder reported in script 05 survives this check nearly intact.

LIMITS OF THIS CLASSIFICATION
  Carrying a recombinase shows a phage can integrate, not that it did so in any particular assay. The converse error is also possible: a phage with no detectable recombinase may still fail to plaque for reasons unrelated to host defense, such as poor adsorption kinetics or a host growth condition. This separates two populations that the plaque assay cannot distinguish; it does not measure lysogeny directly, which would need the assay the dataset does not contain.
