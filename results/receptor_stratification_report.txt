INTERPRETATION AND CONSEQUENCE FOR THE PRIMARY ANALYSIS
  The isolation-host gate is a weak discriminator. It admits 65.7 percent of pairs and raises the lysis rate only from 17.0 to 22.7 percent, an enrichment of 1.34 fold. It assigns 35.9 percent of failed infections to surface exclusion, well short of the 80 to 85 percent that a strict surface-masking account would predict.
  Adding the O-antigen serotype makes the gate far stricter without making it sharper: coverage falls to 12.3 percent of pairs while enrichment stays near 1.39 fold, which is what happens when a filter removes signal and noise in equal measure.
  Enrichment is real in some genera and absent in others. Felixounavirus and Phapecoctavirus separate cleanly, while Vectrevirus and Kayfunavirus, the two largest genera, show essentially none. A gate that works for a minority of genera cannot carry a species-wide claim.

  Two conclusions follow, and they shape the rest of the pipeline.
  First, surface receptor state cannot be reduced to a binary compatibility call from the information this dataset releases. Reporting a hard receptor-compatible subset as though it were validated would overstate what the data support.
  Second, the correct way to remove surface confounding is to stratify on surface state rather than to filter on it. Comparing defense carriers with non-carriers inside the same phage and the same LPS outer core type removes the surface contribution without discarding data and without needing to know which state is permissive. Script 03 therefore reports a conditioning ladder, from an unstratified odds ratio through to one stratified on phage identity and surface state, and treats the gate above as a secondary analysis whose weakness is itself a finding.
